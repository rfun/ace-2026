What is Diwali?

A celebration of light over darkness

Diwali, or Deepavali, means “a row of lamps.” It is one of the most widely celebrated festivals in the world, marking the triumph of light over darkness, good over evil, and knowledge over ignorance.

Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, and some Buddhists celebrate it, each with their own stories and meaning. Families light clay lamps, decorate their doorsteps with rangoli, share sweets, wear new clothes, and gather with the people they love. It falls on the new-moon night of the Hindu month of Kartik, usually in October or November.

The stories

One festival, many stories

Different traditions celebrate Diwali for different reasons. What they share is light.

Hindu

Rama comes home

In the epic Ramayana, Lord Rama returns to the city of Ayodhya after 14 years of exile and his victory over the demon king Ravana. The people light rows of lamps to welcome him home.

Hindu

Krishna defeats Narakasura

Especially in South India, Diwali celebrates Lord Krishna’s victory over the demon Narakasura, which freed thousands of captives and brought light back to the world.

Hindu

Welcoming Lakshmi

Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth and good fortune, is believed to visit homes that are clean, bright, and welcoming. Families light lamps and draw rangoli to invite her in.

Sikh

Bandi Chhor Divas

Sikhs celebrate the “Day of Liberation,” when Guru Hargobind was freed from imprisonment in the early 1600s along with 52 kings. The Golden Temple in Amritsar glows with lights.

Jain

Mahavira’s liberation

For Jains, Diwali marks the day Lord Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, attained moksha, or final liberation. Lamps are lit to honor the light of his teachings.

Nepal

Tihar

In Nepal, the five-day festival of Tihar honors crows, dogs, cows, and siblings. On Kukur Tihar, dogs are garlanded with marigolds and given treats.

The five days

Five days, each one brighter

Diwali is not one night but five days of preparation, worship, feasting, and family.

  1. Day 1

    Dhanteras

    A lucky day to buy gold, silver, or new kitchenware, and to welcome prosperity into the home.

  2. Day 2

    Choti Diwali

    “Little Diwali,” or Naraka Chaturdashi, remembers Krishna’s victory. Homes are cleaned and first lamps lit.

  3. Day 3 · The main day

    Lakshmi Puja

    The darkest night of the month, filled with lamps, prayers to Lakshmi, sweets, and fireworks.

  4. Day 4

    Govardhan Puja

    Remembers Krishna lifting a mountain to shelter villagers from a storm. In Gujarat, it is also New Year.

  5. Day 5

    Bhai Dooj

    A day for siblings. Sisters pray for their brothers’ wellbeing, and brothers give gifts in return.

How Diwali is celebrated

Diyas & lights

Small clay oil lamps line windows, doorways, and rooftops, alongside strings of lights and lanterns.

Rangoli

Colorful patterns made of powder, rice, sand, or flower petals welcome guests at the doorstep.

Mithai & sweets

Boxes of sweets are shared with family, friends, and neighbors. Nobody leaves a Diwali visit empty-handed.

A fresh start

Homes are cleaned top to bottom and decorated, and many people wear new clothes for the festival.

Gifts & gatherings

Families visit one another, exchange gifts, and share big festive meals together.

Fireworks

Sparklers and fireworks light up the night sky on the main evening of Diwali.

Rangoli

Art at the doorstep

Rangoli is a decoration drawn on the floor near the entrance of a home, made to create a warm welcome for guests and for Lakshmi.

It is traditionally made with colored powder or chalk, the same kinds of colors used for Holi in the spring, but people also use rice, sand, flower petals, or paint. Designs are often symmetrical, built out from a center point with petals, dots, and lamps.

  1. Start with a dot or small circle in the center.
  2. Build rings of petals outward, keeping each ring symmetrical.
  3. Fill each ring with a bold color: saffron, magenta, gold, teal.
  4. Finish with a border of dots, and place a diya nearby.

The festive season

Navratri, Dussehra, then Diwali

Diwali caps a season of festivals. First comes Navratri, nine nights honoring the goddess Durga, famous for the joyful Garba and Dandiya dances of Gujarat. On the tenth day, Dussehra celebrates Rama’s victory over Ravana, often by burning giant effigies. About three weeks later, Diwali celebrates Rama’s return home.
